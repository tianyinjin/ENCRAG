1.1 
Manned-Unmanned Teaming in Multi-Domain Operations 
Manned-unmanned Teaming (MUM-T) refers to the coordinated operations of autonomous uncrewed 
systems working along-side humans. It redefines team dynamics with new roles, authority, structures, 
learning, coordination and task allocation processes. It allows multi-agent teams with different types of 
capacities, knowledge and interdependence to be combined across domains, for diverse missions. MUM-T 
can enhance teams’ capabilities, creating a force multiplier, minimizing cost of human risks and deployments 
in operations, while enabling co-learning capabilities. In the following section we will discuss some of the 
elements which make MUM-T challenging, and how AI and M&S can help address these.  
1.1.1 
Autonomous and Unmanned Agent  
NIST defines autonomy in Unmanned Systems (UMS) as their ability to sense, perceive, analyze, 
communicate, plan, make decisions, and execute tasks to achieve specific goals, often with little to no human 
intervention. Autonomy is categorized by Human Independence (HI) and Contextual Autonomous 
Capability (CAC), which takes into account the types of missions, environments, and the level of human 
involvement allowed [3]. In practice, autonomous operations enable UMS to perform tasks assigned by 
human operators or other systems, incorporating capabilities like navigation, targeting, and obstacle 
avoidance. However, it is important to note that the idea of UMS fully replacing humans—either in 
achieving the same mission outcomes or reducing casualties is overly simplistic. Unlike humans, UMS lack 
the flexibility and adaptability needed in dynamic and unpredictable combat scenarios [3]. Broadly, 
unmanned systems are defined as powered platforms without onboard operators, which rely on external 
control units, computers, and communication systems to receive commands, gather data, and execute 
missions [3]. Examples include unmanned ground (UGV), unmanned aerial (UAV), unmanned maritime 
(UMV), unmanned underwater (UUV), unmanned water surface borne vehicles (USV), unattended 
munitions (UM), and unattended ground sensors (UGS). These are increasingly becoming integral 
components of various applications, from military operations to environmental monitoring. These systems 
are typically equipped with a wide array of sophisticated autonomous capabilities, which allow them to 
perform complex tasks such as target identification, detection, and tracking. A key technology that enables
