1.1.2 
Human Factors and Meaningful Control 
Workload is a critical issue; operators managing multiple UAVs or unmanned systems often experience 
performance degradation, particularly during communication-heavy tasks, with increased workload leading 
to reduced trust in autonomous agents [4]-[5]. Trust, defined as confidence in an agent’s ability to support 
goal achievement under uncertainty [6] is vital for collaboration. Lack of trust, driven by low transparency 
and agent reliability, can increase stress and reduce mission effectiveness [7]. Overreliance or underuse of 
autonomous systems may occur based on their perceived reliability, further impacting performance [8]-[9] 
Human Factor studies are important as they explore how transparency, reliability, and task allocation impact 
operator behavior and decision-making in HAT systems. As autonomous agent capabilities are becoming 
more advanced and humanlike, the best method for humans and agents to effectively coordinate is still 
unknown [10]. In order to overcome human factor challenges, some researches have tried modelling human 
capabilities and cognition. However, many challenges come into play when we try to model humans [11]. 
According to the Army Research Laboratory (ARL) there are the eleven gaps in human modelling: 1) 
cognition, 2) decision science, 3) human physiology, 4) human psychology, 5) leadership, 6) morale, 7) 
solider family, 8) solider resilience 9) stress, 10) unit as a complex dynamic, 11) unit cohesion. Thus, those 
components are crucial elements to consider related to huma factors and understanding of human cognitive 
capabilities when it comes of mission and performance. These factors are important to consider for force 
readiness but also when designing intelligent systems for mission orchestration. We are particularly 
interested in decision-making more specifically military decision-making process in the multi-domain 
operating environment [13]. Based on [13] the Military Decision-Making Process (MDMP) consists of 
several key phases, each contributing to the formulation and execution of an effective operational plan (see 
Figure 1). The MDMP begins with the receipt of mission, where the commander receives tasks and provides 
initial guidance on time and resources. During mission analysis, the commander synthesizes guidance, 
higher-level orders, and available information to develop a problem statement, mission statement, and initial 
intent, including identification of key intelligence requirements. COA development follows, generating 
potential operational approaches, which are then war-gamed to assess decision points and assumptions. COA 
comparison evaluates these options to recommend the most feasible course of action. In the COA approval 
phase, the commander reviews and finalizes the chosen option, and the plan is communicated during order 
production to ensure clear transition from planning to execution. In order to test MDMP for force readiness, 
the use of training exercise using simulation and modelling should be necessary.
