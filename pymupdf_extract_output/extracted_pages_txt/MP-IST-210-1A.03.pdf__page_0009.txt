Another structural critique of public benchmarks is also noteworthy here as the modality of these 
benchmarks, being public in nature, can also encourage researchers to overfit to specific datasets rather than 
fostering robust and generalizable solutions. While there is no precise evidence that this is occurring on 
UAV-specific benchmarks, Singh et al. have confirmed the advantage of ‘gaming’ such benchmarks through 
real world experiments on an LLM benchmark, Chatbot Arena, through selective reporting of results. [13] 
They note that ‘undisclosed testing policy permits preferred providers to bias reporting of results’, where ‘a 
small group of preferred providers… test multiple models privately and only submit the score of the final 
preferred version’, and that such ‘selective disclosure violates [the central] unbiased sampling 
assumption...which systematically inflates model rankings, distorting the leaderboard ranking”. In any case, 
when multiple submissions are allowed and some stochasticity is inherent within the results, the best version 
of each developer team’s models rise to the top of the leaderboards and obfuscate a more comprehensive 
view of their overall performance. On UAV-specific benchmarks for researchers with arguably lower stakes, 
the existence of the incentive itself may indicate such activity ongoing – for example, on the premier 
benchmark, the 4th Anti-UAV Workshop, the top 3 winners across the participants in 3 distinct tracks 
(detection, classification, and multi-drone tracking) had each submitted between 8 - 29 entries before 
achieving their distinction, suggesting evidence of such phenomenon in effect. Note that up to 100 
submissions were permitted under this benchmark’s rules. 
2.2.2 
Limitations of Organization and Project-Centric Evaluations of CV Systems 
The above issues of public benchmarks are presumably well-known by organizations seeking to deploy or 
procure AI and CV in their products and services, especially defense, security and intelligence agencies with 
a keen interest in applying these technologies to their vast collection of operational data. However, they face 
another problem of data sensitivity, as well as the often extremely specialized and under-represented nature 
of their use cases, which prevent them from sharing this data in partnerships with broader benchmark 
providers. Hence, the organizations Resaro work with often turn to independent internal evaluations, which 
tend to be project or use-case centric, and are certainly restricted to their own organizations even if there are 
attempts to propagate their use through establishing internal leaderboards or benchmarks. While these 
internal evaluations may be well-tailored for the specific operational requirements of their designers, they 
also present their limitations.  
First, their bespoke nature means that they can be much more resource-intensive and time-consuming, 
hindering agile development and development cycles. Even in the case of the United States, with an 
extremely technologically advanced military, and sophisticated defense industrial base and with many 
notable past AI breakthroughs, testing and evaluation remains a problem that requires long timelines. The 
inaugural director of the DOD’s Joint Artificial Intelligence Center (JAIC) Retired Air Force Lt. Gen. John 
“Jack” Shanahan noted at the virtual event at the Center for New American Security titled “Safe and 
Effective: Advancing Department of Defense Test and Evaluation for AI and Autonomous Systems” [19] 
that the Pentagon “is not well postured yet” for testing and evaluation, or T&E, “of artificial intelligence, or 
any other kind of cutting edge software that requires continuous updating… under the conditions of 
combat… people have a vested interest in coming after and making your models not work the way you want 
them to work. You have explosions, you have fog, you have day, you have night… even though [our past 
models] were trained with operational data, once you put them in the operational environment, sometimes 
they’re just not gonna work the way you want them to work.” He went on to note that the DOD would “have 
got to get used to the fact that these updates [of AI models, to take into account changing operational 
conditions] may be happening in hours and days, not months and years.” The development of each internal
