Another point of consideration in evaluating the completeness of the datasets would be the introduction of 
general image noise under operational conditions, which may manifest through variations in sharpness, 
contrast, edges, entropy, and similarity of the target against its background. To that extent, we developed a 
proprietary metric to consider these manifestations of overall image noise which could make objects hard to 
detect in each image, called the Hard-to-Detect Score (HDS). The HDS takes into account a blended set of 
the above metrics, and produce a single score that would attempt to explain how likely it was that targets 
within said image would be hard to detect on a scale from 0-6; the lower the score, the more likely it is that 
targets within the image would be easier to detect, and the higher the score, the less likely. These scores were 
tested on our curated collections of real-world data across visual data from ground-based, airborne, and 
remote sensing imagery, with input from domain experts, to arrive at a confidence (R2 > 0.6) that it would 
have sufficient explanatory power to indicate if image noise was complicating object detection algorithms on 
said images. In Figure 8 above comparing the IR segment of the dataset (left), and the electro-optical/ visual 
segments (right), we observe that that the IR segments are on average less noisy (mode = 0), while the 
EO/visual segments are noisier (mode = 2); the latter also demonstrates more diversity in the levels of noise 
represented within the dataset. This suggests that any evaluation based on these datasets should account for 
the differences in confidence between the CV system under test under scenarios of noisy IR images. As our 
simulation platform is currently unable to simulate noisy IR data, we opted to apply random noise 
perturbations to a part of the IR dataset to raise the representation of noisy conditions. 
Overall, we weighted the sim dataset towards scenarios with ‘sky’ and ‘foliage’ backgrounds, ‘multiple’ and 
‘small, drones, as well as ‘dark’ lighting scenarios. After creating this simulation data to augment the parts 
lacking in real-world test data, the distribution and resulting confidence intervals are as below (Table 7), 
showing significant improvements in the ‘small-multiple’ category and ‘foliage-dark’ category. Note that in 
so doing, we had to employ some degree of bounded randomization to the parameters in the simulation, 
necessary for enhancing realism and select for instances where the drones would be captured performing 
‘organic’ maneuvers under realistic conditions. These led to natural limitations within the resource 
boundaries of this project in generating significant numbers of foliage-dark scenarios that would still have 
clearly visible drones within the test data, especially as foliage often led to complete occlusion, although the 
‘foliage-dark’ nonetheless showed considerable improvement. Combining all three segments of the dataset, 
we now have a benchmark with sufficient test coverage to test the hypothetical vendors’ models in question, 
and a framework through which we can link the results to our operational objectives through the ASQI.
