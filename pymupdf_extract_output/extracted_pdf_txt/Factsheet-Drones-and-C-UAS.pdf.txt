Defence Industry and Space
Drones and 
Counter-Drone Systems
Why do drones and counter-drone 
systems matter?
Drones, or Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), have become 
indispensable on the modern battlefield, providing real-time 
intelligence, enabling precision strikes, and contributing directly 
to the neutralisation or destruction of enemy personnel, 
materiel and installations. They operate at all levels of 
engagement, from small quadcopters used by infantry units 
to larger systems conducting strategic surveillance or long-
range strikes. This has made drones central to situational 
awareness and command-and-control.
At the same time, their proliferation has created new 
vulnerabilities. Low-cost drones can threaten and destroy 
high-value classic military assets and civilian 
infrastructure. Because they are cheap and easily replaced, 
they give smaller actors disproportionate power. Counter-drone 
systems, or C-UAS, play an essential defensive role: they 
protect airspace, troops, equipment, and critical sites from an 
increasingly diverse range of threats.
DEFENCE 
APPLICATIONS
Drones are redefining the modern 
battlefield, enabling real-time 
intelligence, precision strikes, and 
unprecedented situational awareness
Counter-drone systems have 
become indispensable to detect, 
disrupt, and neutralise hostile 
threats across all domains
Mastering the interplay between 
drones and counter-drone systems 
is key for Europe to safeguard its 
borders and preserve its 
technological edge
The distinction between civil and defence drones is not merely 
technical but also operational. Civil drones perform routine 
tasks, whereas military drones are designed for 
contested, high-risk environments where survivability, 
redundancy and adaptability are critical for mission success.
Civil drones, used for mapping, inspection or delivery, operate in 
controlled environments under predictable conditions. Defence
drones, by contrast, must function in hostile settings where 
communications can be jammed, navigation systems spoofed, 
and air defences active. They require hardened communication 
links, autonomous navigation capabilities, and resilience to 
electronic warfare and cyber interference.
Military drones are also integrated into broader defence
networks, providing real-time data to intelligence, surveillance 
and targeting systems. Their software supports secure 
communications and rapid operational decision-making, rather 
than remote control.

When will EDF project results be translated into military capabilities?
The EU Defence Readiness Roadmap 2030 identifies drones and counter-drone systems as one of the key capability areas 
for strengthening Europe’s strategic autonomy and operational readiness. In this context, the European Drone Defence
Initiative and the Eastern Flank Watch have been launched as flagship projects to reinforce air and border defence, protect 
critical infrastructure, and enhance resilience against unmanned and hybrid threats.
Complementing these efforts, key EDF actions are strengthening EU leadership in this domain by building a connected 
ecosystem that links research, development, and would facilitate scalable production. A total of €1 billion from the EDF and 
its precursor programmes has been dedicated to a wide range of drone-related research and development actions.
Successful project examples include LOTUS, ALTISS, HYBRID, REACT II, E-CUAS and SMALL UAS. The HYBRID project 
focuses on developing a long-endurance, hydrogen fuel cell-powered electric drone. REACT II builds on the results of 
REACT I to advance Airborne Electronic Attack (AEA) capabilities, equipping the EU with critical electronic warfare systems to 
counter drone threats. E-CUAS, the successor to the successful JEY-CUAS initiative, is developing next-generation counter-
drone capabilities by integrating multiple technologies, including passive and active sensors, soft- and hard-kill effectors, 
command and control components, networking and communications infrastructure, cybersecurity features, and interoperability 
data models. Finally, the SMALL UAS project is developing an advanced, AI-driven swarm system composed of unmanned 
aerial systems designed for multi-domain operations.
