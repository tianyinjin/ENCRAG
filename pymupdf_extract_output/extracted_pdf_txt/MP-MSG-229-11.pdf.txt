ABSTRACT  
The growing incorporation of unmanned systems (UAVs, UGVs, USVs, UUVs) within all domains is 
reshaping military operations, from planning to mission execution. NATO regularly demonstrates interest in 
training exercises such as REPMUS (Robotic Experimentation and Prototyping with Maritime Unmanned 
Systems), where the synchronization of hybrid manned-unmanned teams is critical. STAR (Sharing Tasks 
with Autonomous Resources) is an AI-driven tasking tool designed to share mission tasks across manned and 
unmanned systems, supporting planning, tasking, orchestration, and execution of coordinated multi-domain 
operations. STAR integrates multi-modal data, performs capability reckoning (assess capacity for 
autonomous and human agents), and delivers AI-based tasking suggestions to Command and Control (C2) 
systems with human oversight. Through modelling & simulation (M&S) environments like Unreal Engine, 
Cesium 3D geospatial modeling, and JSBSim flight dynamics, STAR enables high-fidelity mission execution 
including representative manned and unmanned vehicles with their appropriate assets and AI-based 
capabilities in realistic scenarios such as underwater protection, air-sea-ground combat, or search and 
rescue. STAR enables Man-Machine Interaction while allowing C2 operators to validate or override the 
tasking recommendation. Achieving tasking accuracy up to 89% using machine learning models, STAR 
shows promise to improve mission readiness and enhance coordination, interoperability, and 
synchronization of effects across joint multi-domain operations.

As unmanned systems play an increasingly prominent role in multi-domain operations, new challenges arise 
in testing and training force readiness. These systems are reshaping warfare, prompting a need to redefine 
roles, responsibilities, interdependencies, and trust within military operations. NATO has prioritized adapting 
to these technological advances without fundamentally altering its operational doctrines. As unmanned

1.1 
Manned-Unmanned Teaming in Multi-Domain Operations 
Manned-unmanned Teaming (MUM-T) refers to the coordinated operations of autonomous uncrewed 
systems working along-side humans. It redefines team dynamics with new roles, authority, structures, 
learning, coordination and task allocation processes. It allows multi-agent teams with different types of 
capacities, knowledge and interdependence to be combined across domains, for diverse missions. MUM-T 
can enhance teams’ capabilities, creating a force multiplier, minimizing cost of human risks and deployments 
in operations, while enabling co-learning capabilities. In the following section we will discuss some of the 
elements which make MUM-T challenging, and how AI and M&S can help address these.  
1.1.1 
Autonomous and Unmanned Agent  
NIST defines autonomy in Unmanned Systems (UMS) as their ability to sense, perceive, analyze, 
communicate, plan, make decisions, and execute tasks to achieve specific goals, often with little to no human 
intervention. Autonomy is categorized by Human Independence (HI) and Contextual Autonomous 
Capability (CAC), which takes into account the types of missions, environments, and the level of human 
involvement allowed [3]. In practice, autonomous operations enable UMS to perform tasks assigned by 
human operators or other systems, incorporating capabilities like navigation, targeting, and obstacle 
avoidance. However, it is important to note that the idea of UMS fully replacing humans—either in 
achieving the same mission outcomes or reducing casualties is overly simplistic. Unlike humans, UMS lack 
the flexibility and adaptability needed in dynamic and unpredictable combat scenarios [3]. Broadly, 
unmanned systems are defined as powered platforms without onboard operators, which rely on external 
control units, computers, and communication systems to receive commands, gather data, and execute 
missions [3]. Examples include unmanned ground (UGV), unmanned aerial (UAV), unmanned maritime 
(UMV), unmanned underwater (UUV), unmanned water surface borne vehicles (USV), unattended 
munitions (UM), and unattended ground sensors (UGS). These are increasingly becoming integral 
components of various applications, from military operations to environmental monitoring. These systems 
are typically equipped with a wide array of sophisticated autonomous capabilities, which allow them to 
perform complex tasks such as target identification, detection, and tracking. A key technology that enables 
these functions is computer vision, with algorithms such as YOLO (You Only Look Once) and

1.1.2 
Human Factors and Meaningful Control 
Workload is a critical issue; operators managing multiple UAVs or unmanned systems often experience 
performance degradation, particularly during communication-heavy tasks, with increased workload leading 
to reduced trust in autonomous agents [4]-[5]. Trust, defined as confidence in an agent’s ability to support 
goal achievement under uncertainty [6] is vital for collaboration. Lack of trust, driven by low transparency 
and agent reliability, can increase stress and reduce mission effectiveness [7]. Overreliance or underuse of 
autonomous systems may occur based on their perceived reliability, further impacting performance [8]-[9] 
Human Factor studies are important as they explore how transparency, reliability, and task allocation impact 
operator behavior and decision-making in HAT systems. As autonomous agent capabilities are becoming 
more advanced and humanlike, the best method for humans and agents to effectively coordinate is still 
unknown [10]. In order to overcome human factor challenges, some researches have tried modelling human 
capabilities and cognition. However, many challenges come into play when we try to model humans [11]. 
According to the Army Research Laboratory (ARL) there are the eleven gaps in human modelling: 1) 
cognition, 2) decision science, 3) human physiology, 4) human psychology, 5) leadership, 6) morale, 7) 
solider family, 8) solider resilience 9) stress, 10) unit as a complex dynamic, 11) unit cohesion. Thus, those 
components are crucial elements to consider related to huma factors and understanding of human cognitive 
capabilities when it comes of mission and performance. These factors are important to consider for force 
readiness but also when designing intelligent systems for mission orchestration. We are particularly 
interested in decision-making more specifically military decision-making process in the multi-domain 
operating environment [13]. Based on [13] the Military Decision-Making Process (MDMP) consists of 
several key phases, each contributing to the formulation and execution of an effective operational plan (see 
Figure 1). The MDMP begins with the receipt of mission, where the commander receives tasks and provides 
initial guidance on time and resources. During mission analysis, the commander synthesizes guidance, 
higher-level orders, and available information to develop a problem statement, mission statement, and initial 
intent, including identification of key intelligence requirements. COA development follows, generating 
potential operational approaches, which are then war-gamed to assess decision points and assumptions. COA 
comparison evaluates these options to recommend the most feasible course of action. In the COA approval 
phase, the commander reviews and finalizes the chosen option, and the plan is communicated during order 
production to ensure clear transition from planning to execution. In order to test MDMP for force readiness, 
the use of training exercise using simulation and modelling should be necessary.

1.1.4 
AI for Decision-Support in MUM-T  
The use of AI has gained interest on the battlefield on the tactical level. At both the operational and strategic 
tiers of military operations, artificial intelligence significantly supports data analysis and planning efforts. Its 
capacity to handle vast amounts of information enables AI to assist in decisions such as force deployment 
and selecting plans that most effectively advance strategic objectives [14]. This suggests considerable 
potential for AI to play a larger role in high-level military decision-making in the future [14]. While AI is not 
yet widely deployed in this capacity, certain systems have demonstrated superior performance compared to 
human military personnel at the tactical level, indicating strong potential for expanded AI involvement in 
strategic military decision-making in the future. AI enables the synthesis of a large amount of data in short 
amount of time, which might help counter human biological limitations. These systems employ AI-driven 
targeting and control mechanisms capable of processing information and executing actions within fractions 
of a second [14]. AI could similarly be applied to decision-support when it comes to counter rapidly 
emerging threats, such as hypersonic missiles, where the response window is extremely limited, although no 
such defenses are currently known to exist. The growing presence of autonomous weapons on the battlefield 
highlights AI’s tactical significance, as its speed and responsiveness in dynamic combat scenarios can 
provide a decisive advantage over conventional systems. At higher levels of warfare, including operational 
and strategic planning, AI contributes substantially to data analysis and decision support. Its ability to 
process large volumes of information allows it to inform choices such as force deployment and selection of 
plans that best advance strategic objectives. It can help solve difficult questions such as “where to commit 
forces, what plan would get the closest to the strategic objective and more [14]. Thus, this can help support 
the MDMP during mission to re-adjust COA based on constant data streaming, dynamic changes in MUM-T 
capabilities, and situational awareness.  
2.0 STAR (SHARING TASKS WITH AUTONOMOUS RESSOURCES) 
STAR is designed in order to facilitate the OODA loop for decision-making in the three phases of missions: 
i) Mission pre-operations - via supporting war gaming simulation and determination the best course of action 
based on if-then scenario, ii) in-operation - as it can help support decision-making surrounding dynamic 
tasking since often plans and orders given prior to the mission might change due to change of events, iii) 
STAR could also help support after-action analysis to better prepare and support future mission, help assess 
current states of each agent capability to perform their tasks and duties, it could be used to calibrate training / 
performance of human and autonomous agent through modelling and simulation. STAR could facilitate the 
readiness of forces by providing a system capable of plugging into a simulator which can help generate and 
put in place mission operations, launch team, and test and evaluate different strategies while receiving AI 
recommendation towards tasking of MUM-T. STAR is composed of several components which are 
described in Section 2.1. The three main modules presented are i) multi-modal pipeline; ii) capability 
reckoning, iii) dynamic tasking module. Each of those three can be fed from simulation and modelling data 
which enables the system to be tested on specific use-cases which are of interest and can help gather data and 
potential behavioral insight towards how each manned or unmanned agent might behave in the mission.

1) Multi-Modal Pipeline and Pre-Processing: This module processes situational awareness data, 
extracting relevant features for dynamic tasking. It filters and engineers the data, transforming it into 
actionable mission parameters, like trajectory calculations from GPS data. It also allows 
customization based on vehicle types, mission objectives, and operational needs, supporting a 
variety of mission scenarios. 
2) Reckoning: STAR evaluates which agent—human or autonomous—is best suited for a task by 
analyzing both past and current behaviors. It uses data from the multi-modal pipeline, such as 
mission status and environmental sensing, to determine the interdependence between agents. This 
helps identify capacity gaps and whether agents can act independently or need support. 
3) Dynamic Adaptive Tasking: This module focuses on task allocation for manned-unmanned 
teaming (MUM-T). It takes into account pre-operation prerequisites, doctrine, and the commander’s 
requirements while enabling real-time adjustments. It analyzes the interdependence among team 
members (both human and autonomous) and recommends which agent should lead or support based 
on the operational context. The commander can either accept or modify these recommendations and 
adjust the level of autonomy as needed.

2.1.1 
Modelling and Simulation 
Modelling and simulation enables answering several challenges for STAR. It first enables to simulate the 
actual mission operations, which involve many factors, actors and events. The first benefit is that it enables 
creating situational awareness data required for STAR to understand the operational context. This allows 
collecting multi-modal data (sensors, human, environment). The second benefit is that it allows co-hosting 
manned and unmanned agent systems which can both benefit from this simulated world where on one hand 
humans-in-the-loop can be immersed in a close to reality environment, which helps get human behavioral 
data such as performance, mental workload or stress which could be close to the real environment. Using 
M&S such as Unreal enables the creation of diverse scenarios such as search and rescue missions in water 
environment; ISR mission in MDO operations, or critical infrastructure operations such as NATO 
Experiment. On the other hand, it enables the autonomous vehicles such as UAV and UUV to be trained and 
tested in the same world or environment as human agent. Diverse plug-ins can be used to enhance the 
realism of the simulation and fulfil the need of data generation. Vigilante offers a platform for integrating 
manned and unmanned vehicles in realistic simulations, supporting a range of MDO operations ranging from 
air, ground, projectile and sea types of assets. These assets can be controlled via human operator within the-
loop simulation or can be completely autonomous. Based on the use-case, STAR can enable python scripts 
and different AI capabilities which can be integrated within those assets such as deep reinforcement learning 
algorithms, computer vision, path planning and more. This allows STAR to have realistic assets composed of 
battlefield capabilities used in operations. Secondly, one essential plug-in for the air domain is JSBSim. It is 
an open-source flight dynamics software used for realistic ranging from 737, B17, f-15, f-16 and more. It is 
widely referenced in both academic and industry research, with applications in reinforcement learning for 
flight control, as seen in studies like [15] and [16] (JSBSim on GitHub). Cesium provides 3D geospatial 
visualization, crucial for simulating large-scale operational environments and real-time geospatial awareness 
in MDOs. It can be integrated with game engines like Unreal for enhanced terrain modeling and operational 
planning (Cesium Unreal Plugin). Cesium World Bathymetry integrates bathymetric data, vital for naval, 
submarine, and amphibious operations, adding realism to underwater and coastal simulations (Cesium World 
Bathymetry). These tools collectively offer a comprehensive simulation ecosystem, essential for training, 
mission planning, and operational execution in modern military contexts.

3.1 
Use-Case: Critical Undersea Infrastructure Protection  
Hybrid threats in the maritime domain are evolving, with covert attacks on undersea infrastructure becoming 
a major concern. The Nord Stream pipeline sabotage and Baltic Connector pipeline damage highlight the 
challenges in distinguishing between accidental and deliberate actions, especially when attackers use civilian 
vessels or covert tactics. The rise of Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) and specialized sabotage 
vessels, such as those used by Russia, further complicates protection of critical undersea infrastructure 
(CUI). At the 2023 NATO Vilnius Summit, allies established the Maritime Centre for the Security of Critical 
Underwater Infrastructure within MARCOM, focusing on deterring and defending against hybrid threats 
targeting energy and communication networks. This includes addressing incidents like the Nord Stream 
attack, the damage to the Baltic Connector pipeline and data cables between Finland and Estonia in October 
2023, and ongoing threats to Norwegian gas pipelines and fiber-optic cables, underscoring NATO’s 
commitment to securing Europe’s vital undersea assets.  
3.2 
Modeling & Simulation and System Integration 
This use-case models the defense of critical undersea infrastructure against a range of asymmetric threats in a 
contested maritime environment. The Red Team simulates a series of offensive actions aimed at disrupting 
or destroying vital subsea assets, including communication cables, offshore platforms, and sensor networks. 
These actions include the deployment of UUVs from submarines or surface vessels to sabotage subsea 
cables, cyberattacks to compromise control systems and exfiltrate data via UUVs, signal jamming using 
surface drones or UUVs to disrupt communication, and direct physical assaults on offshore platforms. In 
response, the Blue Team employs a layered defense strategy that integrates advanced assets for detection, 
interdiction, and neutralization of threats. The defense relies on UAVs, such as the Camcopter S-100, for 
aerial surveillance and signal jamming detection, while Iver-3 UUVs perform subsea patrols to inspect 
cables and intercept hostile UUVs. Submarines are deployed for long-range sonar detection and 
counterattacks, supported by swarm drones for persistent coverage and jammer localization. Frigates provide 
surface surveillance and anti-submarine warfare (ASW) capabilities, while RHIBs enable rapid deployment 
for personnel extraction and anti-sabotage operations. The use-case examines the interplay between these 
assets to evaluate force readiness and response capabilities in the face of cyber, physical, and electronic 
warfare tactics aimed at critical undersea infrastructure. The use-case uses the same location as Rempus and 
Dynamic messenger which occurs in Troia, Portugal.

3.3 
Dynamic Tasking of MUM-T in MDO  
The algorithm presented here addresses a dynamic, energy-constrained decision-making problem for 
autonomous agents operating in complex environments. The agent, modeled with capabilities for target 
detection, observation, and engagement, is tasked with navigating towards a final destination while 
optimizing energy consumption. The system operates by reading a JSON configuration file, which details the 
agent’s energy levels, capabilities, and environmental factors such as observation and engagement range. 
The agent’s reasoning process is driven by continuous updates of its energy state, simulating time 
progression and movement through a series of predefined positions determined during mission planning. As 
the agent moves, it computes the energy required to reach subsequent positions or the final destination, 
adjusting its route if it detects insufficient energy to proceed. At each decision point, the algorithm checks 
whether the agent’s current energy level is adequate for reaching the target positions, considering both 
observation and engagement capabilities. If the agent’s energy falls below the required threshold to reach the 
final destination, it reassesses its route, discarding positions that are unreachable. If the agent has enough 
energy to proceed, it makes decisions based on the optimal route and energy consumption. This decision-
making is extended to multi-agent scenarios, where the algorithm allows for the sharing of information 
regarding target observations and the assignment of tasks based on the least energy-expensive agent. In cases 
where observations are unreachable by any agent, they are flagged and excluded from the task list. The 
algorithm’s ability to dynamically adjust to changing energy levels and environmental conditions, while 
considering agent capabilities and task prioritization, showcases its potential for real-time, energy-efficient 
decision-making in autonomous systems and MUM-T coordination.

4.0 CONCLUSION 
Through the use of M&S STAR has been able to train and test different mission with an AI capable of 
reaching 72% [17] 89% [18] 88.1% [19]. This study demonstrated a proof-of-concept implementation for the 
integration of modeling and simulation into NATO’s force readiness framework, specifically in the context 
of MDO. The potential of such a tool is underscored by NATO initiatives like the Innovation Continuum, 
which seeks to advance Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR). Effective tasking and 
coordination of MUM-T remain significant challenges; however, the STAR system offers a promising 
solution by enabling comprehensive training and evaluation of MDO scenarios. This approach helps both 
manned and unmanned platforms identify operational strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities for 
optimization. Looking ahead, future research will focus on refining STAR’s ability to integrate performance 
metrics for evaluating team effectiveness. By incorporating such metrics, the system can provide reflective 
feedback that drives continuous improvement, enhancing team efficiency and interoperability. In parallel, the 
STAR tasking algorithm will be further developed to enhance its predictive capabilities, enabling more 
accurate dynamic tasking and mission execution. The inclusion of expert feedback into the tasking process 
will foster a “teacher-student “ relationship between the operator and the system, promoting trust, 
transparency, and the explainability of AI-driven models which could evolve over time where the AI can be 
capable of assisting the human as a team member when human is overwhelmed or incapacitated due to 
cognitive degradation. This will ultimately strengthen decision-making in C2 systems, ensuring more 
informed and reliable operational choices to facilitate the MDMP and OODA loop. Additionally, research 
will explore the refinement of human meaningful control mechanisms within STAR, empowering operators 
to actively engage with and steer dynamic tasking scenarios through the recommendation algorithm. This 
balance between human vs AI decision-making is crucial for ensuring that the system remains adaptive to 
evolving operational needs. By enhancing the human-machine collaboration, STAR has the potential to 
significantly improve NATO’s readiness, enabling a more agile, resilient, and responsive force capable of 
navigating the complexities of modern, rapidly changing operational environments.
