Miroslav Kratky, Jan Farlik, Josef Casar and Vadim Stary 
University of Defence, Kounicova 65, 602 00 Brno 
CZECH REPUBLIC  
Miroslav.Kratky@unob.cz 
ABSTRACT  
Despite the fight against unmanned vehicles is nothing new, the arrival of new technologies - that are easily 
accessible for the wider population – there has been arising new problems. The deployment of small, 
unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) by paramilitary organizations during conflicts all around the world has 
become a reality, non-lethal “paparazzi” actions have become a common practice, and thus, the fight 
against unfriendly users of unmanned systems is on the top agenda for both military and security bodies. The 
basic prerequisite for direct and successful defence against attacking UAVs is their prompt and sufficiently 
accurate detection. The general outputs obtained from calculated, simulated, and experimentally detected 
values confirmed, that the most effective detection should be realised by multispectral sensor systems. 
1.0 INTRODUCTION 
The producers of commercial drones compete for potential customers, and they innovate and improve their 
products constantly. These innovations continuously improve flight parameters such as flying range, speed, 
load capacity, and autonomous activity algorithms or artificial intelligence capable of transporting the drone 
through a number of obstacles [1]. Like any technology, drones can be misused. The aim of this article is to 
inform about the results of a study that examined the detectability of commercially available drones. The 
authors of the study want to contribute to a greater awareness of drone misuse and offer defence options.  
Governments aiming to protect their citizens from the consequences of UAV misuse have to deal with this 
threat very seriously. This is due to the continuous development and innovation in the field of commercial air 
vehicles and the constant struggle between potential aggressors and victims.  
The risk of successful unmanned aerial systems (UAS) misuse is considerably increasing, and defence 
effectivity is decreasing, for example, in the following ways [2], [3], [4]: 
• 
UAV is able to fulfil its task through autonomous flight, even without GPS; 
• 
swarm of UAVs can attack a target; 
• 
UAS is able to recognize a victims’ face and can aim crowds of people as well; 
• 
UASs have more remote control channels [15] and remote control options; 
• 
UASs can be used to paralyze civilian air traffic control. 
However, the victims of UAV misuse are not limited to government institutions or infrastructure. The 
victims of UAS misuse are more often individuals or companies. These private entities need to be 
protected as well. 
Successful air defence against UAVs should include the following activities: 
• the evaluation of threats and risks for a person, company, infrastructure, or state interests and the 
anticipation of vulnerability (Long-Term Threat Assessment and Anticipation); 
• infrastructure adjustment against unwanted UAS operations (Infrastructure Assessment); 
• surveillance of the surrounding area or eventually the entire airspace and UAS detection 
(Surveillance); 
• acquisition of additional UAS data during flight and risk assessment of its behaviour (Tracking and 
Imminent Threat Assessment); 
• rogue UAS identification and elimination (UAS Target Engagement);

Radar detection 
The main goals of testing and calculation were to determine the targets radar cross-section (RCS) and 
afterward the possibilities of their detection, localisation, tracking and identification

multispectral field through “combined sensors”.

2.2 
Radio frequency detection 
Radio-frequency analysis of UAS signals is now very common in UAS detection. The principle is based on 
the evaluation of the signal transmitted by the control station (RC controller signals—“uplink”) or the signal 
transmitted by the UAV (“downlink”), e.g., telemetry data and video signals. 
Popular commercial drones use WiFi frequencies (2.4 or 5.8 GHz). In accordance with national frequency 
allocation tables (e.g., in Czech Republic, that of the National Radio Frequency Agency—NARFA), other 
frequencies can be also used, e.g. UHF 433 or 434 MHz. National laws also regulate the maximum output 
power of a transmitted signal.  
One of the most important aspects in the detection and identification of UAS is the used data protocol. RF 
detectors are designed based on knowledge of the system topology and structure, primarily knowledge of the 
transport layer. For precise identification, a database (library) of UAV frequencies and protocols is needed. 
A library allows a user to detect and identify the airborne target (drone) “online” based on its data signals. 
The disadvantage of this approach is the possibility of detection and identification for only the drones with 
known parameters (which are included in the database). Detection of non-standard drones in urban 
environments may not be successful. 
The main aim of the experiment was to evaluate the abilities and the possibility of RF drone detection by 
commercial RF detection devices using allowed frequencies. 
We used for experiments an RF detection device produced by Dedrone Inc. [USA & Germany], the 
DroneTracker Event Kit in Demo configuration; this kit consists of a Drone Tracker Multi Sensor and a 
DroneTracker RF Sensor. The multi sensor contains an RGB camera and a pair of microphones too. 
Measurement was performed at distances from 70 m to 1.7 km, and successful/unsuccessful detection and 
identification were evaluated.

2.3 
Infra-Red detection 
The method described here is based on other characteristic attributes of the UAV. The infrared spectrum is 
part of the electromagnetic spectrum, with a wavelength from 760 nm to 1000 μm. The surveillance of the 
aerial area in the IR domain is used mainly for the detection of low altitude aerial targets, and it is part of an 
additional surveillance method whereby “blind” areas in the local air picture (LAP) are covered. 
The thermal imaging camera (FLIR A40M) connected to PC and the appropriate control and evaluation 
software were used for this experiment. For evaluation of the thermal image from the camera, ThermoCAM 
Researcher Professional 2.9, ThermoCAM Explorer 9.9, and MATLAB r2011a were used. 
The laboratory experiments consisted of two parts. The first part was focused on measurement of a thermal 
image of the isolated UAV electric motor, and the second part was focused on a thermal image of the entire 
UAV hovering in the laboratory.

2.5 
Acoustic detection 
As much as optical detection, the acoustic one can be done similarly using human senses – ears, and with the 
aid of technical acoustic devices.  
Acoustic detection by human senses. The series of UAV detection experiments using human ears in the 
real environment were performed in the same place, time and conditions like the Human optical detection 
was done (see above).  
The structure of these experiments was a little simplified: The UAVs were flying away from the evaluators 
who did knew the time and the direction of the flight. In a real situation, the direction of the flight would 
probably be the reverse one and acoustic observer will not exactly know the flight azimuth and altitude. 
Acoustic detection using technical devices. This set of experiments was performed in a real environment. 
The TAROT F650 quadcopter and the DJI S800E hexacopter were the representatives of the mini UAV 
category. The UAV noise was recorded by microphones. These data were post-processed and evaluated 
according to the noise level of the background. 
The experiment condition was as follows: 
• 
climate zone and place: temperate; Czech Republic; 
• 
weather and temperature: sunny, 5 °C; 
• 
wind speed: 4 ms-1; 
• 
humidity: 70%; 
• 
background noise level: 55 dB (measured by SONY F-V120 device); 
• 
UAV distance from the sensor post: 8 m; 
• 
UAV flight altitude: 5 m AGL; 
• 
flight method: 
o 
stabilized altitude hold (constant motor RPM); 
o 
manoeuvre (variable motor RPM). 
Noise data were acquired by the SONY F-V120 microphone and post-processed in MATLAB r2011a. 
During post processing, data were transformed from time to frequency domain. 
3.0 RESULTS 
The main aim of the previous experiments, related study and then this paper was to verify, clarify, and 
summarize the real-time detection of commercial drones. Real-time UAV detection is essential for possible 
counter-action or elimination. Human senses without technical devices are the least effective method of 
detection. However, in a terminal phase of UAV flight, human senses are usually the main means for 
weapon aiming and drone elimination. 
The first detection sentinel line is usually executed by X-band radars, which can provide early warnings. The 
effectivity of this kind of system significantly depends on the skills and training of radar operators’ crew. 
However, the availability and usage of radar are limited, and radars are used mostly by military or air traffic 
control organizations. At present, several studies about those methods have been published, and the research 
is in progress [33], [34], [35], [36]. 
The average RCS of the evaluated UAVs was in the range from 0.05 to 0.2 m2 (in certain directions, the 
value of the RCS peak was 0.5 - 0.75 m2). Measured values correlate with values from similar experimental 
measures [37].

Radars, which are currently in operational use, are usually able to find a flying UAV (made “scan”), but they 
are not able to “track” this type of target continuously, especially at longer distances, or can only do so for 
a limited time, intermittently, with interruptions, etc. The probability of detection therefore depends on the 
target RCS, terrain configuration, and other aspects. Generally, we can say, that radar and IR detection is 
possible at distances of units kilometres. 
Acoustic detection is strongly depending on the distance from a signal source and on the background noise 
level as well.

hexacopter was 92 dB. The frequency peaks for the quadcopter were 22.86, 302, 969, 2113, 2551, 3756, and

4.0 DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 
The results of the study show that X-band radars are usually able to detect LSS targets (micro and mini 
UAV) reliably at distances about 3000 m. However, detection is mostly in the form of radar scans or plots 
and not radar tracks. Based on these results, we recommend focusing on multispectral detection with a 
combination of radar, IR, visual, acoustic, radio, etc. Initial detection (scan, plot) is executed by X-band 
radar (or a radio receiver), and more precise detection at closer distances is achieved by other methods. Once 
initial detection and localization of the UAV is achieved, further action can be taken (lethal or non-lethal 
elimination). 
Authors performed several experiments with standard radars (not specialized to detect small RCS objects), 
however we do not have mandate to publish them in this paper. Radar, that is not primarily designated to 
detect small RCS objects, could be used just as the early warning or alarm device. This early warning may be 
triggered through the scan or several scans or short track on the radar scope before the track disappear due to 
the radar threshold parameters. Authors admit that it would not be effective to implement radars, which are 
not designed to detect small RCSs, to multi-sensor fields. Authors also think that producers should work on 
innovations to cope with such disadvantage. There are some radars now, for example C-RAM radar Arthur, 
which would be suitable for UAV detection when innovated. 
Experiments in the IR, visual, and acoustic domains achieved approximately equal detection limits. All of 
these domains (IR, visual, and acoustic) are appropriate for terminal UAV detection at closer distances (up to 
300 m). However, this distance is usually not sufficient for the non-autonomous reaction procedures (a UAV 
with a flight speed of about 10–15 ms-1 can pass this distance within 20–30 s). For this reason, we 
recommend a C-UAV approach and the implementation of autonomous defence systems with a maximum 
reaction time up to 20 s [42], [43].  
For the crucial objects, complex defence a combination of various types of sensors and the corresponding 
real-time data exchange with a command and control system (C2) or with a surveillance control system is 
necessary in non-military applications. Such a system autonomously fuses the data from multiple sensors and 
evaluates possible threats. The operator (the person in the loop concept) chooses which effectors should be

5.0 CONCLUSIONS 
This paper covers several years of research and supports the design concept of future defence and protection 
systems against UAS threats. Together with the development of radar for LSS targets, there is a great 
potential for the development of robust and reliable systems that are able to detect and eliminate UAV 
threats. The key attribute of the suggested system is its modularity. Potential users (military, air traffic 
control, security services, etc.) can adjust the system to their needs. However, many conceptual and technical 
aspects of the C-UAV systems still need to be concerned. Of course, a general solution that reflects all 
possible situations does not exist - hence the phrase, “there is no silver bullet.” 
Hypothesis, confirmed by this study defines a detection area of up to 2000 m and a terminal area of up to 300 
m around a guarded location. These are key areas for sensor network installation and for the use of counter 
UAS equipment - effectors. 
The last phase of the work will consist of an analysis of current detection methods of small-sized targets, 
from mini or micro UAS categories, across the entire alert system. This last step concludes the logical link 
among the three basic pillars of air defence—sensors, assessment systems (command and control systems), 
and effectors (lethal or nonlethal engagement against UAS).
