ABSTRACT Drones have become integral in modern warfare, and their evolution towards greater autonomy
is inevitable. This study explores the trajectory towards intelligent, minimally human-dependent military
drones, detailing the necessary technological advancements. We simulate drone reconnaissance operations to
identify and analyze emergent challenges. This study delves into various technologies critical for enhancing
drone intelligence, with a focus on reinforcement learning based on object detection, and presents practical
implementation examples to illustrate these advancements. Our findings confirm the significant potential for
enhancing the intelligence of military drones, paving the way for more autonomous and effective combat
solutions.
INDEX TERMS
Intelligent military drones, reconnaissance drone, reinforcement learning, autonomy,
simulation, object detection.
I. INTRODUCTION
D
RONES have been recognized as essential forces
through various proven instances in recent conflicts
such as the Russian Invasion of Ukraine and the Armenia-
Azerbaijan War. Currently, the majority of combat-ready
drones are remotely controlled. Although there is some level
of automation in drones, operators are still required due to
technical and ethical issues. From a tactical perspective, the
best advantages of drones are “low cost” and “mass deploy-
ment.” However, these two advantages only work when the
drone can be controlled without operators, that is, when the
drone is intelligent.
Autonomous drones alone are not a novel concept, as
extensive research has already been conducted. For instance,
we live in an era where unmanned drones are used for deliv-
eries and search and rescue missions [1], [2], [3]. However,
can civilian intelligent drone technology be used directly for
military purposes? Our answer is “no,” because military drone
operations show clear differences from civilian drones in the
following situations. First, the environment in the military
is more complex than that in the civilian sector. Consider
when Tesla is on Autopilot on an unpaved road how often
a driver must intervene. Military operations do not occur on
‘‘well-paved roads.’’ Moreover, military operations involve
missions assigned in arbitrary locations. Second, countless
enemy counteractions accompany military operations. These
counteractions include both active and passive denial, with
active denial involving interception attempts and passive de-
nial including concealment and deception. Such enemy ac-
tivities increase the complexity of the problem. Third, due to
the military’s uniqueness and security, there is a lack of data
related to military operations. For example, there is a shortage
of aerial view data of tanks and Transporter Erector Launchers
(TELs), which are used targets of object detection. Fourth,
safety and ethical aspects need to be considered when a mili-
tary intelligent drone performs a mission. An intelligent drone
lacking stability during operations can result in unpredictable
behaviors, leading to indiscriminate casualties and mission
failure. As an ethical consideration, even though overall drone
operation becomes intelligent, there is a need for the concept
that the final attack decision is made by humans. In other
words, the key consideration should not be whether drones

II. LITERATURE REVIEW
Intelligent drones integrate artificial intelligence technolo-
gies to be autonomous and optimize drone functions and
operations. These drones possess capabilities beyond simple
remote control, being able to sense their environment and
respond autonomously to perform various tasks. Studies are
ongoing to utilize these advanced functionalities, but publicly
accessible information is limited. In military documentation
that is available to the public, especially regarding mixed
manned and unmanned systems, intelligent drones are often
mentioned as one player among many [4], [5], [6], [7], [8],
[9], [10]. This means that the discussion isn’t fully focused or
detailed specifically on military drones.
Papers focusing on intelligent military drones can be
broadly categorized into two types. Those centered on engi-
neering and those that are not. The latter type includes papers
discussing policies, military strategies, and ethics related to
intelligent military drones. There are diverse opinions in these
papers regarding military intelligent drones. From a negative
viewpoint, concerns are raised about ethical issues, the un-
predictability of AI, military ethics, and stability problems
[11]. Conversely, from a positive perspective, some argue
that intelligent drones, when used appropriately, can be more
objective and ethical in performing tasks than humans, who
might be influenced by emotions such as revenge or anger
[12]. Regarding making attack decisions with drones, many
papers recommend that humans should be the ones to make
the final decision. Yet, some advocate that drones could inde-
pendently make such decisions if equipped with an ethical
system [13]. While these papers provide a comprehensive
overview of various aspects of military intelligent drones,
they often lack an in-depth engineering perspective on how
to enhance the intelligent capabilities of these drones.
On the other hand, papers on intelligent military drones
from an engineering perspective in the military domain are
few [14], [15] and often lack sufficient reflection of military
specificity. In contrast, research on the intelligence of civilian
drones is progressing, as illustrated in Figure 1, which we
have personally compiled and summarized. From this point
on, the review will be based on Figure 1.
• The applications of artificial intelligence in civilian
drones are being researched for drones equipped with
sensors and systems such as Electro-Optics (EO), Infra-
Red (IR), Laser Range Finder (LRF), Light Detection
and Ranging (LiDAR), Radio Detection And Ranging
(RADAR), Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS),
Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), Ultrasound, and Syn-

researched area in the field of drone intelligence, where
deep learning and reinforcement learning are extensively
applied [23], [24], [25].

drones apply multi-agent reinforcement learning for
tasks like target tracking [27], network resource alloca-

focuses on applying AI technologies to enhance network
systems for drone-to-ground or drone-to-drone data ex-
change. In a more detailed examination of security, there
is research utilizing reinforcement learning to determine
the optimal trajectory for UAVs to avoid jamming [31].

of wireless signals, leading to the obstruction of com-
munication channels. Furthermore, studies are apply-
ing PCA (Principal Component Analysis), CNN (Con-
volutional Neural Network), and LSTM (Long Short-

are manipulated to mimic or imitate specific devices
or users, misleading the system. Adversarial attacks,
designed to confuse machine learning models, especially
deep learning models, involve adding subtle changes
to model input data, leading to incorrect predictions or
classifications [33]. Research on adversarial attacks and
defenses against deep learning-based drones includes
studies on adversarial patch attacks [34]. These attacks
significantly decrease the image detection accuracy of
drone networks.
• Just as network issues are considered constraints in
drone operations, the field of power and energy manage-
ment also faces challenges. In this area, there are stud-
ies on using reinforcement learning to improve energy
efficiency during drone navigation [35], and research
into automated recharging systems for drones that are
battery-operated and autonomous [36].
• Finally, the embedded systems installed in drones have
performance limitations in terms of computational ca-
pacity, memory, and power consumption. Due to this, the
integration of heavy deep learning models is restricted,
leading to research on lightweight deep learning models
[37].
A. THE CONTRIBUTION OF RESEARCH
Our study’s primary contribution lies in providing intuition
for the intelligence of military drones from an engineering
perspective, based on a high level of understanding in the
military domain. The details are as follows.
• First, we construct a scenario from a military domain
perspective, focusing on a reconnaissance mission car-
ried out by a military intelligent drone. This scenario
describes the process of the drone scouting a specific
sector, detecting and tracking an enemy, and provid-
ing information with high detection reliability to the
decision-maker responsible for attack. Based on this
scenario, we create a custom simulation environment.
• Second, we examine the engineering technologies re-
quired for the operation of military intelligent drones.
These technologies encompass a wide range, from tradi-
tional rule-based and Proportional-Integral-Differential
(PID) control to recent advancements in artificial intel-
ligence. Specifically, we conduct experiments on data
augmentation using Generative Adversarial Network
(GAN) and the training of object detection algorithms,
as well as reinforcement learning for drone movement.
The examination of these engineering technologies ulti-
mately provides insights into how reinforcement learn-
ing can be applied at specific stages to enhance drone
intelligence, presenting solutions to the challenges en-
countered.

Military drones are classified according to their purpose of
use into reconnaissance, attack, deception, electronic warfare,
and as targets [38], [39]. In this case study, we focus on the
intelligence of reconnaissance drones. The drone in the case
study is modeled after the drone of the Korean Poongsan com-
pany, as shown in Figure 2. The model can perform a variety
of tasks depending on the applied modules. However, in this
study, a drone equipped with reconnaissance modules is used.
The module includes sensors and systems such as cameras,
LRF, GNSS, and others. For specification assumptions, it is
presumed that the drone is capable of being equipped with an
object detection and reinforcement learning neural network.
A. SCENARIO
The scenario assumes specific conditions and objectives
for research purposes. An intelligent reconnaissance drone,
equipped with the capability to detect enemies, is deployed
for the mission. The drone’s task is to locate and track enemy
tanks, identified as key targets, in a designated sector during
clear daytime weather conditions. After tracking, the drone
provides information about these tanks to the final decision-
makers for attack planning. It is assumed that a communi-
cation system has been pre-established between the drone
and the decision-makers, allowing for data transmission and
reception. The sector assigned for the drone’s reconnaissance
contains trees and structures, offering potential concealment
or camouflage for the tanks. Additionally, there is a risk of the
drone being shot down if it flies too low, due to the possibility
of unexpected anti-aircraft fire from the enemy within the
sector.
B. TECHNOLOGY APPLIED IN THE SCENARIO
Given such a scenario, what specific technologies could be
applied to make the drone intelligent? Let’s examine this
based on Figure 3. The technologies mentioned here are
not yet at a stage where they can be implemented in real-
world environments. Therefore, it is important to note that
all subsequent content discussed has been implemented and
tested within the Unity simulator.
1) Firstly, as drones need "eyes" for intelligence, we apply
the lightweight algorithm YOLOv4-tiny, considering
the limited computational speed and memory of em-
bedded systems. This choice is also influenced by the
fact that YOLOv4-tiny is a relatively recent algorithm
that is compatible with the Unity simulator.
2) Drones, with cameras facing downward from above,
require top-view images of tanks for effective object
detection. However, most publicly available online re-
sources predominantly feature frontal photos of tanks.
Getting pictures of military equipment from specific
angles is challenging due to security reasons. There-
fore, this study uses synthetic data from Unity and
augmented real images. The training and validation
data are divided into three categories, which include
real images, synthetic data, and augmented data derived
from real images. Among these categories, the real
images consist of 1180 tank photos taken from various
angles, including 30 aerial view tank photos, which
are similar in size to those observed by drones. We
use 700 images of Unity synthetic data and create 400
augmented images from 30 real aerial view photos of
tanks. These 400 augmented images are further divided
into two groups where the first consists of 150 images
transformed through methods like geometric resizing,
Gaussian blur, image inversion, color inversion, and
grayscale conversion, and the second comprises the re-
maining 250 images augmented using Alias-Free Gen-
erative Adversarial Networks, known as StyleGAN3
[40]. The test dataset uses 100 aerial view real images
not included in the training and validation data. As
shown in Table 1, the best performance is achieved
when real images, Unity synthetic data, and augmented
data are combined.

4) Maintaining a specific altitude also utilizes PID con-
trol. The altitude is measured using an LRF sensor,
and PID control is applied to reduce the difference
between the target value, which is the desired altitude,
and the output value, which is the current altitude
[42]. Maintaining a certain altitude is advantageous as
it ensures the consistent size of the observed object.
Maintaining a uniform object size aids in better de-
tection. This is because, generally, if an object detec-
tion model is trained primarily with small objects for
detecting smaller items, it tends to perform poorly in
detecting larger objects. Conversely, if it is trained with
larger objects to detect bigger items, smaller objects are
not detected as effectively [43]. This trade-off is why
having a consistent object size helps in improving the
overall detection accuracy.
5) In line with this, as illustrated in Figure 5, PID con-
trol is also implemented for tracking when an enemy
tank is detected. During the drone’s reconnaissance and
tracking phases, the camera is consistently oriented
to face straight down. Consequently, the center of the
camera’s image (0.5, 0.5) corresponds to the drone’s
two-dimensional location. When an enemy is detected
at a position like (0.8, 0.8) in the camera view, the
discrepancy from the drone’s location at (0.5, 0.5) is
considered the error. PID control is then engaged to
minimize this error, enhancing tracking accuracy.

This implies that relying solely on the detection neural
network is not the best strategy. Therefore, moving the
drone and adjusting the angle of the camera attached to
it to secure a line of sight on the object can be another
way to enhance the confidence level. However, could
such movements be addressed with a sort of rule-based
approach? Considering the constraints of maintaining
a certain altitude and the drone’s requirement to look
downwards from the air, resolving this issue with a
rule-based approach is not as straightforward as it may
seem. Let’s think of a situation where, if something
like a tree is hiding a tank, the drone has a rule to fly
away from that tree and turn its camera to look at the
tank. For this strategy to work effectively, it’s crucial
to recognize the kinds of objects that could conceal the
tank and figure out which portion of the tank is hidden.
However, training the system to segment and identify
every potential concealing object, such as various trees
and structures, poses a significant challenge, especially
with limited computational resources. Additionally, the
task of detecting a tank that is partly masked is com-
plex, as it requires pinpointing which part of the tank
is covered. While it might look like an easy problem
to solve with basic rules from a human perspective,
for a drone, the situation is much more complex and
not as easy to handle. This is exactly the point where
advanced technology is needed to realize the concept
of intelligence.
Therefore, this paper proposes using reinforcement
learning to solve the issue of securing a line of sight on
incompletely concealed objects and increasing object
confidence levels. For this, we utilize images where
objects have been detected along with various vec-
tor observations to autonomously control the drone’s
movement and the camera angle. In the next chapter,
we delve deeper into the fundamental concepts of re-
inforcement learning, conduct experiments, and then
discuss the results.
C. REINFORCEMENT LEARNING
In reinforcement learning, an agent in the environment, which
is the space where the agent is located, selects actions based
on the current situation information, known as the state. The
environment then changes in response to these actions, and
the agent receives rewards as a result. The goal of reinforce-
ment learning is to maximize the cumulative rewards [46].
In this study, the environment is partially observable from the
agent’s perspective. When the drone scouts for enemies in the
Unity simulator space, it doesn’t initially know the location of
the enemies. It observes only part of the environment using its
camera, not having complete information about the enemies
from the start. This is different from fully observable games
like chess or Go, where the positions and movements of all
pieces are known. In partially observable situations, the term
‘observation’ is used instead of ‘state’ to describe the current
situation information. The following sections introduce the
components of reinforcement learning and the algorithms
used.
1) Environment
In this research, the reinforcement learning aims to achieve
advanced intelligent behaviors in scenarios occurring after
relatively basic tasks like waypoint reconnaissance and en-
emy tracking. Therefore, the reinforcement learning envi-
ronment concentrates on the drone’s autonomous actions to
enhance the detection confidence level after tracking a tank,
especially when the confidence level is low. As depicted in
Figure 7, at the start of learning, the drone is positioned
above a partially obscured tank. The drone receives visual
input through a red camera located on its underside, which is
essential for the detection process. This red box represents the
drone’s "eye." The grey camera and the large grey square are
used to observe the drone’s behavior for verification purposes
and are not directly involved in the learning process.
To prevent the model from overfitting to a specific envi-
ronment during training, domain randomization is employed
in each episode. This process involves randomly varying
elements within the simulation environment, which helps the

3) Algorithms, hyper-parameters
In this research, the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
algorithm [50] from the Unity ML-Agents package [51] is
used for reinforcement learning. Unity offers a set of tools

specific attributes, is a policy-based algorithm widely used
in reinforcement learning. Here, policy essentially means the
set of rules or strategy the agent follows to decide its actions
based on the environment. PPO is known for its gradual and
steady approach in updating these policies. It uses a clipping
technique to limit the extent of these updates, ensuring that
the new policy doesn’t veer too far from the old one. This
balance helps maintain stability in learning. The hyperparam-

IV. CONCLUSION
This paper offers an engineering perspective within the mil-
itary field on using drones with object detection capabilities
for military purposes. This is explored through a case study
based on specific scenarios. When thinking about using smart
drones in the military, it’s important to consider factors such
as the complexity of the environment, efforts by enemies to
interfere, lack of data, the need for stable operation, and the
provision of information for making attack decisions. The
environmental complexity in this scenario is highlighted by
the task of identifying enemies concealed under structures
or trees in varied terrains, including urban and mountainous
regions. In fulfilling this mission, the drone is programmed
to maintain a certain altitude to enhance the detection of
hidden enemies and to safeguard against unforeseen threats.

data generated by the Unity simulator alongside real image
data that has been augmented, utilizing both to enhance the
overall dataset. For operational stability, the drone’s flight,
